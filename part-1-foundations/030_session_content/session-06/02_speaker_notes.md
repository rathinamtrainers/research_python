## Segment 2 · Lists — creation, methods, slicing, `del` (0:08–0:30)

### Slide 5 — Working with lists

Right. Lists. This is Python's everyday container — the one you will reach for more than any other, probably by a factor of ten. So let's build a really solid foundation here.

### Slide 6 — What is a list?

A list is an ordered, changeable collection of items, and you write it with square brackets, with the items separated by commas. Look at the example: `tasks` equals a list of three strings — "write report", "buy milk", "call bank". That's it. That's a list. Now let me pull out the four things that actually define it, because each one matters. First: a list is **ordered**. The items stay in the positions you put them in. "write report" is at position zero and it will still be at position zero tomorrow unless *you* move it. That sounds obvious, but not every container works this way — next week you'll meet sets, which don't promise you order at all. Second, and this is the big one: a list is **mutable**. That word means changeable — a list can be modified in place, after it's been created. Compare that to strings, which you met in Week 3: strings are immutable, they can never be changed, and every string method hands you back a brand-new string. Lists are the opposite, and that difference is going to be the whole middle of today's session. Third: a list can hold **anything** — strings, numbers, booleans, even other lists, and you can mix types in the same list. Python won't stop you. In practice, though, you'll almost always keep one *kind* of thing in a list, because that's what makes your code predictable. And fourth: the things you already learned on strings work here too. `len` of tasks gives you three. And the little word `in` asks "is this in there?" — `"buy milk" in tasks` gives you `True`. One last thing to notice, and it's a genuine source of beginner confusion, so let me name it now: square brackets do two different jobs. When you write them with commas inside, you're *making* a list. When you write them after a name, you're *indexing into* one. Same symbol, two jobs. You'll get used to it within a day.

### Slide 7 — Growing a list

So let's grow a list. There are three methods for adding things, and the difference between them is worth getting right the first time. The first is `append`, and it adds exactly **one** item to the end. `tasks.append("call bank")` — the list is now one longer. Now here's the thing about `append` that I want you to hold on to: it adds one item, even if that item happens to be a list. If you append a list to a list, you get a list *inside* your list — a single item that happens to be a list. That's occasionally what you want and usually a surprise. The second method is `insert`, and it's `append`'s more precise sibling: you give it a position and an item, and it puts that item there, shuffling everything else along to make room. `tasks.insert(0, "pay rent")` puts "pay rent" right at the front. Read `insert(i, x)` as "put x *before* whatever is currently at index i" — that phrasing will save you off-by-one errors. And the third is `extend`, which is the one people mix up with `append`. `extend` takes an *iterable* — a list, a tuple, a string, anything you can loop over — and it adds **each item from it, individually**. So `tasks.extend(["water plants", "book flights"])` makes the list two items longer, not one. If you'd used `append` there, you'd have made it one item longer and that item would have been a list of two strings. Append adds the box; extend adds the contents of the box. And now the line at the bottom, which is your first glimpse of today's real theme: all three of these change the list **in place**, and all three return `None`. They don't hand you a new list back. They reach into the list you already have and modify it. Hold that thought — we're going to come back to it hard in about twenty minutes.

### Slide 8 — Shrinking a list

Now let's take things out, and there are two main ways, and the difference between them is one of the most useful distinctions in the whole list API. The first is `remove`, and `remove` works **by value**. You say `tasks.remove("buy milk")` and Python goes hunting through the list for the first item equal to "buy milk" and takes it out. Two things to know about it. It removes the **first** match only — if "buy milk" is in there three times, you've removed one of them and two remain. And if the value isn't in the list at all, you don't get a polite `False` back — you get a `ValueError` that stops your program. So `remove` is for when you're confident the thing is there. The second way is `pop`, and `pop` works **by position**. `tasks.pop()` with no argument takes the **last** item off, and — this is the good bit — it *returns* it to you. Look at the output: it hands back `'book flights'`. So `pop` is "take it out and give it to me", which is exactly what you want when you're processing a queue of work. You can also give `pop` an index: `tasks.pop(0)` takes from the front and hands you back `'pay rent'`. And there's `clear`, which just empties the whole thing. So let me put the distinction in one sentence, because this is the sentence to remember: **`remove` takes a value and gives you nothing back; `pop` takes an index and gives you the item back.** Get those two straight and you'll never fumble a list again.

### Slide 9 — Finding & counting

Two quick methods for looking things up. `index` tells you **where** something is: `tasks.index("call bank")` gives back `1`, meaning it's sitting at position one. Like `remove`, it finds the **first** match, and like `remove`, if the value isn't there at all it raises a `ValueError` rather than returning anything helpful. And `count` tells you **how many times** something appears — `tasks.count("call bank")` gives back `1`. `count` is the safe one: if the item isn't there, you get `0`, no exception, no drama. So there's a nice little idiom hiding here — if you want to look something up safely, you can check `count` first, or use the `in` keyword, rather than letting `index` blow up in your face. Now, the note at the bottom of the slide is a genuine wrinkle that I want to warn you about, because it will bite you and the error message won't be obvious. `index` does accept two optional extra arguments, a start and a stop, so you can search just part of the list. But you can only pass them **positionally**. If you try to be helpful and write `tasks.index("x", start=0)` with `start` as a keyword — which is exactly what a reasonable person would do, because the documentation writes them in square brackets like optional keyword arguments — you get a `TypeError` telling you that `list.index()` takes no keyword arguments. It's an old corner of Python that predates the friendlier conventions you learned last week. So: positionally only. And while we're here — one more thing that will confuse you if you go and read the docs. The official documentation calls that first parameter `value`, but the error messages call it `x`. They disagree with each other. That's not you misreading it; that's genuinely inconsistent in Python itself.

### Slide 10 — Reordering & copying

Three more methods, and then we'll go and use all of this. `sort` puts the list in order — smallest to largest, or alphabetically for strings. `reverse` flips it end to end. And notice what I've written next to both of them: they work **in place**, and they return `None`. They rearrange *this* list. They do not hand you back a sorted copy. This is so important that we're going to come back to it at the end of the session as the last gotcha of the day, so let me just plant the flag now: `nums.sort()` changes `nums` and gives you nothing. Next: `sort` takes two arguments, `key` and `reverse`, and both of them are **keyword-only** — which is a phrase you learned last week, so you already know exactly what it means. It means you must write `sort(reverse=True)`; you cannot write `sort(True)`. If you try, Python tells you flatly that `sort() takes no positional arguments`. And last, `copy`, which hands you back a **new** list with the same contents. Right now that probably looks like the least interesting method on this slide. By the end of the next twenty minutes it will look like the most important one. Trust me and hold on.

### Slide 11 — Indexing & slicing

Good news here: you already know this. This is exactly the notation you learned in Week 3 on strings, and it works identically on lists, because both of them are *sequences* and Python is beautifully consistent about sequences. `tasks[0]` gives you the first item. `tasks[-1]` gives you the last one — negative indices count backwards from the end, so minus one is the last, minus two is the second to last. And `tasks[1:3]` gives you a slice: items one and two. Remember the rule from strings — the start is included, the stop is **not**. One to three means one and two. And here's the important line at the bottom: indexing gives you **one item**, but slicing gives you a **new list**. A slice is always a fresh list containing copies of the references — the original is completely untouched. That's a very useful property and, as you'll see shortly, it's also a sneaky way to copy a list. The whole `start`, `stop`, `step` grammar carries over too, so `tasks[::-1]` reverses a list into a new list, just like it reversed a string.

### Slide 12 — Slice assignment

Now here's something you have never seen before, because strings simply cannot do it. You can assign **to** a slice. Look at what happens. We start with four tasks. Then I write `tasks[1:3] = ["ring the bank"]` — I'm putting a list on the *right* of the equals, and a *slice* on the left. And Python takes out items one and two, and drops in the contents of that replacement list in their place. We had four tasks; now we have three: "pay rent", "ring the bank", "water plants". Two items went out, one came in, and the list quietly resized itself to fit. That's the part that surprises people: the replacement does **not** have to be the same length as the slice you're replacing. Longer, shorter, empty — it all works, and the list just grows or shrinks around it. Assign an empty list to a slice and you've deleted that range. And I want you to notice *why* this is possible, because it's the whole theme of today: this works because lists are **mutable**. You genuinely cannot do this to a string. Try `name[0:2] = "Hi"` and Python will tell you a `str` object does not support item assignment, exactly as you saw back in Week 3. Lists say yes. Strings say no. That difference is not a detail — it's the personality of the two types.

### Slide 13 — The `del` statement

And the last piece of list surgery is `del`, which is a *statement*, not a method — you'll notice there's no dot, you write `del` and then the thing you want gone. And it removes **by position**. Let me walk the example, which is lifted from the official Python tutorial. We start with a list of six items. `del a[0]` removes the first one. `del a[2:4]` removes a whole *slice* — items two and three go away in one stroke. `del a[:]` deletes every item, leaving you with an empty list — the list is still there, it's just empty now. And then the last line does something different in kind: `del a`, with no brackets at all, deletes the **name** `a` itself. The variable is gone. Mention it after that and Python will tell you `a` is not defined. So how do you choose between `del` and `pop`? Here's the clean way to think about it: they both remove by position, but `pop` **hands the item back to you**, and `del` just deletes it. So if you want the item, `pop`. If you just want it gone, `del` says what you mean more clearly. And one small point of trivia that's actually useful: `del a[:]` and `a.clear()` do exactly the same thing, so use whichever reads better to you. Right — that's the whole mechanical toolkit for lists. But there's an idea underneath all of it that we now have to face properly, because it's the thing that separates people who *use* Python from people who get *confused* by Python.

## Segment 3 · Mutation, aliasing & Demo 11 (0:30–0:55)

### Slide 14 — Mutation & aliasing

So. This next bit is the heart of today's session. If you remember nothing else from these two hours, remember this. This is the trap that catches everyone — beginners, and especially experienced developers coming from other languages who think they already know what an equals sign does.

### Slide 15 — Mutable means it changes

Let's put the two behaviours side by side, because the contrast is the whole lesson. On top: strings are immutable. When you call `name.upper()`, the original string is not touched at all — you get a *new* string handed back to you, and if you don't catch it in a variable, it just evaporates. That's why in Week 3 I kept saying "string methods return new strings" — you had to assign the result to something or it was lost. Underneath: lists are mutable. When you call `tasks.append("x")`, nothing is handed back — it returns `None`. Instead, the list you already have is reached into and changed. And notice the consequence, because it's the exact opposite of the string habit you just spent three weeks building: with a list you do **not** write `tasks = tasks.append("x")`. If you do, you'll assign `None` to `tasks` and destroy your list. You just write `tasks.append("x")` on its own line and the work is already done. Now, the sentence at the bottom of the slide is the mental model I want to install, and it's worth saying slowly, because everything else follows from it. **A name doesn't hold a list. A name points at one.** Don't picture a variable as a box with a list sitting inside it. Picture it as a label with an arrow, and the arrow points off to a list that lives somewhere in memory. The name and the list are two separate things. Once you see it that way, the next slide is obvious. Until you see it that way, the next slide is baffling.

### Slide 16 — Aliasing — two names, one list

So let's watch it happen. I make a list `a` with two tasks in it. Then I write `b = a`. Now — pause on that line and ask yourself honestly what you think it did. Almost everyone reads `b = a` as "make me a copy of a and call it b". It does not do that. It does not copy anything. What it does is copy the **arrow**. Now `b` points at exactly the same list that `a` points at. Not a twin. Not a duplicate. The *same list*, with two labels stuck on it. And so when I do `b.append("call bank")`, and then ask Python to show me `a` — look at that — `a` has three items now. I never mentioned `a`. I appended to `b`. But there was only ever one list, so of course `a` sees it, because `a` and `b` were always pointing at the same thing. And the line that proves it beyond argument is `a is b`, which comes back `True`. Now, `is` is a word we haven't used much yet, so let me be precise: `is` does not ask "do these look the same?" — it asks "are these the *same object*?" Same thing, same place in memory, one object with two names. And it says `True`. This is called **aliasing**, and here's why I'm making such a meal of it. Nothing here is broken. Python is doing precisely what it promised — it copied the arrow, because that's all an equals sign ever does. But if you *expected* a copy, this behaviour looks like a haunting. You'll change one list and watch a completely different variable on the other side of your program mutate, and you'll lose an evening to it. So please, take this away with you: **`b = a` never copies a list.**

### Slide 17 — The copy fix

And the fix is delightfully simple, once you know that you need it. If you actually want a second, independent list, you have to *ask* for one, and there are three ways to ask. `a.copy()` is the clearest and says exactly what it means. `a[:]` is a full slice from beginning to end, and we said a slice always builds a new list, so slicing the whole thing gives you a copy — you'll see this a lot in older code. And `list(a)` builds a new list out of anything you can iterate over, which includes a list. All three do the same job here; pick whichever reads best. And look at the proof: `c is a` is now `False` — genuinely two different objects — while `c == a` is `True`, because they hold the same contents. And there's the distinction that trips people up, so let me nail it: **`is` asks "same object?", `==` asks "same contents?"** They are completely different questions and today is the day they come apart in your head. Now, one word of honesty before we move on, because I don't want to teach you something you'll have to unlearn. What all three of these give you is a **shallow** copy. That means you get a new outer list, but the items inside it are still the same objects, shared between both lists. For everything we're doing today — lists of strings, lists of tuples of text and numbers — shallow is exactly right and you never need to think about it. If you ever have a list *of lists* and you copy it and then start mutating the inner lists, you'll discover the shallow-copy limit, and there's a tool called `deepcopy` waiting for you. That's a Part 2 conversation. Today, shallow is your friend. Right — enough talk. Let me show you every bit of this live.

### Slide 18 — Let's mutate a list, live.

Let me share my screen and we'll build this together in the REPL, because this is one of those things that you have to *see* moving before it really lands.

### Slide 19 — Watch this — Demo 11

So here's what I'm about to do, and I'd like you to watch rather than type — you'll rebuild all of this yourself this week from the recording, and right now I want your eyes on the screen, not on your keyboard. First I'll build up a to-do list and put every one of those methods through its paces — `append`, `insert`, `extend`, `remove`, `pop` — and we'll watch the list change shape underneath each one. Then I'll slice-assign a whole range away in a single line. And then we get to the main event: I'm going to write `b = a`, append to `b`, and then show you `a` — and you'll see `a` change, even though I never touched it. That's the aliasing bug, live. Then I'll fix it with `copy` and show you the two lists finally moving independently. And I'll finish with `sort` versus `sorted`, which we'll talk about properly at the end of the session. Here we go.

### Slide 20 — The aliasing bug

So there it is, exactly as promised, and I want to sit on this screen for a moment because this is the slide to screenshot. I made a list and called it `a`. I wrote `b = a` — one short, innocent line. I appended to `b`, and only to `b`. And then I asked for `a`, and `a` has grown a third item. Now, I know some of you are looking at that thinking Python has done something sneaky. It genuinely hasn't. There was only ever **one list** in this entire example. `b = a` didn't build a second one — it just hung a second label on the first. And `a is b` confirms it: `True`. Two names, one list. Every mutation through either name is visible through both, because both arrows point at the same place. Say it with me: two names, one list.

### Slide 21 — What you just saw

Let me pull that demo together into the five things worth carrying out of it. First: list methods change the list in place and return `None` — so you never write `tasks = tasks.append("x")`, and if you do, you've just thrown your list away and replaced it with nothing. Second: `pop` hands you back the item it removed and works by index, while `remove` works by value and gives you nothing back. Third, and this is the one: `b = a` is an **alias**, not a copy. An equals sign copies the arrow, never the list. Fourth: when you genuinely want a separate list, you ask for one — `.copy()`, or `[:]`, or `list(a)`, all three work. And fifth: `is` compares identity, `==` compares contents, and today is the day those stopped being the same question. Now let's meet the other container, which solves this whole problem in a completely different way — by refusing to change at all.

## Segment 4 · Tuples, unpacking, nesting & comparison (0:55–1:17)

### Slide 22 — Tuples

So if a list is the container that changes, a tuple is the container that won't. And that turns out to be a feature, not a limitation.

### Slide 23 — What is a tuple?

Here's a tuple: `task = (1, "write report", False)`. Round brackets this time, items separated by commas. And you read from it exactly like a list — `task[1]` gives you `'write report'`. Indexing, slicing, `len`, `in`, looping — all identical to a list, because it's a sequence and Python is consistent. But watch what happens when I try to *change* it. `task[0] = 9`, and Python stops me flat: `TypeError: 'tuple' object does not support item assignment`. And if that error message rings a bell, it should — it's almost word for word the one you got in Week 3 when you tried to change a string. Same family of error, same reason: this object is **immutable**. There's no `append` on a tuple. There's no `remove`, no `sort`, no `del` of an item. Once you've built it, that's what it is, forever. So the question you should be asking is: why on earth would I want a container that can't change? And here's the answer, which is the practical heart of this slide. Use a **list** when you have many of the same kind of thing, and the collection grows and shrinks — a list of tasks, a list of names, a shopping basket. Use a **tuple** when you have **one record with a fixed set of fields** — an id, a title, and a done-flag; or an x and a y coordinate; or a red, green and blue colour. The give-away is this: in a list, position doesn't carry meaning — item five is just whatever happened to land there. In a tuple, **position *is* meaning** — slot zero is the id, and it will always be the id. And because it can't be changed, you can hand a tuple to any function in your program and know with total certainty that it'll come back the way you sent it. No aliasing bug is even *possible* with a tuple, because there's nothing to mutate. That's not a restriction; that's a guarantee.

### Slide 24 — The one-element trap

Now, a small and genuinely surprising thing, and I'm giving it its own slide because it catches absolutely everybody at least once. Look at the first example: `type((1))` — one, wrapped in round brackets — and Python says `<class 'int'>`. That's not a tuple. That's just the number one. But `type((1,))` — with a trailing comma — and *now* Python says `<class 'tuple'>`. So here's the rule, and it's the opposite of what your eyes tell you: **it is the comma that makes a tuple, not the brackets.** The brackets are almost always optional. Python sees `(1)` and reads those brackets the way it reads brackets in arithmetic — as ordinary "do this bit first" grouping, exactly like `(2 + 3) * 4` from Week 2. There's no comma, so there's no tuple, and you just get the number back. And the corollary is fun: `1, 2, 3` with no brackets whatsoever is a perfectly good tuple. That's why you can write `return x, y` from a function and get a tuple back — you're not returning two things, you're returning one tuple, and the comma built it. The only genuine exception is the empty tuple, which you write as a bare pair of brackets, because with nothing in it there's nowhere to put a comma. So: single-item tuple, don't forget the trailing comma. It looks like a typo. It's load-bearing.

### Slide 25 — Packing & unpacking

Now we come to the thing tuples are really *for*, and it's the bit that'll make your code look properly Pythonic. When I write `task = (1, "write report", False)`, that's called **packing** — I've packed three values into one tuple. And then this line — `tid, title, done = task` — is **unpacking**, and it's lovely. Three names on the left, one tuple on the right, and Python hands out the values in order: `tid` gets `1`, `title` gets `"write report"`, `done` gets `False`. One line, three variables, no square brackets, no `task[0]`, no `task[1]`. And now compare that to the alternative you'd otherwise write — three separate lines pulling things out by index, and every one of those index numbers is a chance to make a mistake and a puzzle for whoever reads it next. Unpacking says what you *mean*. The one rule is that the counts must match. If you put two names on the left and a three-item tuple on the right, Python raises a `ValueError` and tells you exactly what went wrong: `too many values to unpack (expected 2, got 3)`. And if you go the other way — three names, two values — it says `not enough values to unpack (expected 3, got 2)`. Those are two of the friendliest error messages in the language; they tell you precisely what it wanted and precisely what it got. And here's the payoff line at the bottom, which is the thing you'll actually use every day: this is *why* you can write `for tid, title, done in tasks:` and have all three names appear ready-made on every turn of the loop. That's not special `for`-loop magic — it's just unpacking, happening once per item.

### Slide 26 — Star-unpacking & the swap

Two more unpacking moves, and they're both small and both delightful. The first is star-unpacking. `first, *rest = [10, 20, 30, 40]` — and the star means "soak up everything that's left over". So `first` gets `10`, and `rest` gets `[20, 30, 40]`. Now one detail that surprises people, so let me point right at it: `rest` comes back as a **list**, even though you might have unpacked a tuple. The star always collects into a list. And the star doesn't have to go at the end — you can write `*most, last` to grab everything but the final item, or `head, *mid, tail` to take the two ends and let the middle collect itself. There can only be one star, which makes sense, because with two Python wouldn't know where one ended and the next began. And then the second move, which is the one you'll show off to your friends: `a, b = b, a`. That's it. That's how you swap two variables in Python. No temporary variable, no `temp = a` dance. If you're coming from another language, you almost certainly wrote a three-line swap with a temp variable this week, and here it is in one line that reads like plain English. And the reason it works is worth knowing, because it's not a special-cased swap feature — it's just packing and unpacking meeting each other. The **right-hand side is evaluated completely first**, building the tuple `(b, a)` out of the current values, and only *then* does Python unpack that tuple back into the names on the left. The old values are safely captured in the tuple before anything gets overwritten. It's not a trick; it's just the two things you've already learned, working together.

### Slide 27 — A list of tuples

And now let's put the two containers together, because this combination is the single most common data shape in beginner Python, and it's the exact shape your homework is built on. Look at it: `tasks` is a **list**, and each item in it is a **tuple**. So the list holds many tasks and it can grow and shrink all day long — that's the mutable part. But each individual task is a fixed three-field record — an id, a title, and a done-flag — and it cannot be quietly corrupted. That's the immutable part. A mutable collection of immutable records. And that is not an accident or a compromise; it's a genuinely good design, and it's how a great deal of real Python is written. Mutability exactly where you need it, and nowhere else. And then look at the loop underneath, because this is where all of today's threads come together in one line: `for tid, title, done in tasks:`. On every turn, Python takes the next tuple, unpacks it into those three names, and hands them to you ready to use. No indexing. No `task[0]` and `task[1]` and trying to remember which slot the title was in. Just three well-named variables that say what they are. That single line is what today has been building towards.

### Slide 28 — Sequence comparison

One last concept before we go and demo all of this, and it's a quiet one that explains something you'd otherwise find mysterious. You can compare tuples and lists with the ordinary comparison operators. `(1, 2, 3) < (1, 2, 4)` comes back `True`. So how does Python decide? It compares **item by item, left to right**, exactly like alphabetical order in a dictionary. It looks at the first items — one and one, tied, keep going. Second items — two and two, tied, keep going. Third items — three versus four, and three is smaller, so the first tuple is smaller, and Python stops right there and answers. The **first difference decides everything**, and nothing after it is even looked at. And if one sequence runs out while everything so far has been equal? Then the **shorter one is smaller** — that's why `(1, 2)` is less than `(1, 2, 3)`. Exactly like "car" comes before "cart" in a dictionary. And here's why I'm telling you this at all, rather than it being trivia: this is precisely *why* `sorted()` can sort a list of tuples without you explaining anything to it. It just compares them pairwise, using this rule. So a list of `(id, title, done)` tuples sorts by id first, and then by title where the ids tie, and it does that for free. Right — let's go and build a task out of a tuple, live.

## Segment 6 · `.sort()` vs `sorted()` + Q&A (1:40–1:50)

### Slide 33 — `.sort()` vs `sorted()`

So. Python gives you two ways to sort, they're named almost identically, and they behave completely differently. Let's be very clear about both. `sorted(nums)` is a **function**. You hand it a sequence, and it hands you back a **brand-new sorted list**, and it leaves your original completely alone. Look at the screen: `sorted(nums)` gives `[1, 2, 3]`, and then when I ask for `nums` again, it's still `[3, 1, 2]` — untouched, exactly as it was. And `nums.sort()` is a **method**. It reaches into `nums` and rearranges it in place, and it hands you back **nothing at all**. After it, `nums` is `[1, 2, 3]`. Both correct, both useful, totally different. And now the trap at the bottom of the slide, which I promise you every single Python programmer has fallen into at least once, usually in their first month. Somebody writes `nums = nums.sort()`. It looks so reasonable! It reads like "sort nums and put the answer back into nums". But `.sort()` returns `None` — so what you have actually done is sort your list and then immediately throw it away and replace it with `None`. And your program doesn't crash on that line. It crashes three lines later when you try to loop over `nums` and Python tells you that `None` is not iterable, and you go and stare at the wrong line for twenty minutes. So here's your rule of thumb, and it's a good one: **if you want a sorted copy, use `sorted()`. If you want to sort in place, call `.sort()` on its own line and don't assign it to anything.** And notice how neatly that lines up with everything else today: methods that mutate return `None`. Same story as `append`, same story as `remove`, same story as `reverse`. It's one consistent rule, and once you've seen it you'll never be caught by it again.

### Slide 34 — Sorting by a key

And one last piece, which you need for your homework, and which is where last week's lambdas finally pay off. By default, sorting a list of tuples uses that item-by-item comparison we just discussed, so a list of tasks sorts by id first, because the id is in slot zero. But what if you want them sorted by **title**? You tell `sorted` what to sort *by*, using the `key` argument: `sorted(tasks, key=lambda t: t[1])`. And read that lambda out loud — "given a task `t`, use `t[1]`" — which is the title. So Python takes each tuple, runs it through that little function to find the value it should sort on, and orders them by *that*. Look at the result: "buy milk" now comes before "write report", ordered by title, not by id. That's the whole idea of `key`. Two footnotes before we stop. `.sort()` only exists on lists, because only a list can rearrange itself — but `sorted()` will take *any* sequence, a tuple, a string, anything you can iterate, and always gives you back a list. And if you try to sort a mix of types that can't be compared — numbers and strings together — you get a `TypeError` saying that `<` is not supported between those two types. Python won't guess what you meant, and honestly, that's the right call. We go much deeper into `key` in Week 8, so for now you just need this one shape, and it's exactly the one your lab needs.
