## Segment 2 · Comprehensions — list, set, dict, filters, conditions, nesting (0:08–0:32)

### Slide 5 — Comprehensions

So: comprehensions. This is the headline feature of today, and I want to introduce it by showing you a problem you already have, rather than by showing you syntax. Let me show you a loop you have written many, many times already in this course.

### Slide 6 — The loop you keep writing

Look at this on screen. I want a list of the squares of the numbers one to five. So I create an empty list called squares. Then I loop: for n in range one to six. And inside the loop I append n times n to my list. Three lines. And it works perfectly — there's nothing wrong with this code. But I want you to look at it with fresh eyes for a second and notice how much of it is *bookkeeping* rather than *idea*. The actual idea I had in my head was just five words: "the square of every n". That's it. But to express those five words I had to create an empty container, remember its name, write a loop, and then call append on the right list with the right value. Three of those four things are chores. They're the price Python is charging me to express a very simple thought. And Python's designers looked at this and said: this is such a common shape — build a new collection out of an existing one — that we should give it its own syntax. And they did. It's called a comprehension, and it lets you write just the idea.

### Slide 7 — The list comprehension

Here it is. Squares equals, open square bracket, n times n, for n in range one to six, close square bracket. One line. And I want you to read it the way it's designed to be read, which is left to right, in plain English. It says: give me n times n — that's the *what* — for each n — that's the *each* — in range one to six — that's the *from where*. What, each, from where. That's the whole grammar. And notice the brackets: those square brackets on the outside are Python telling you what kind of thing you're going to get back. Square brackets mean you get a list. That'll matter in a few minutes when we change them. Now, if you run this you get exactly the same answer as the loop: one, four, nine, sixteen, twenty-five. Identical result. But look at what's gone: no empty list to create, no append to call, no container name repeated three times. Just the idea. And here's the thing I really want you to take away — a comprehension is not a clever trick or a code-golf shortcut. It's the *normal*, expected way to write this in Python. If you hand a Python developer the three-line loop from the last slide, they'll ask you why you didn't use a comprehension. This is the default.

### Slide 8 — Adding a filter

Now let's make it more useful, because very often you don't want every item — you want only some of them. And for that you put an if at the *end* of the comprehension. Look at the example: evens equals, bracket, n, for n in range ten, if n modulo two equals zero. And that gives you zero, two, four, six, eight. Read it left to right again, and it still works as English: give me n, for each n in range ten, if n is even. The if on the end acts as a filter — it's a gate. Each item comes along, the condition is tested, and if the condition is false that item is simply dropped and never makes it into the list. I've put the long version underneath so you can see they're the same thing: the empty list, the for, the if, the append. Four lines becomes one, and honestly, I find the one-liner easier to read, not harder, because the filter sits right next to the thing it's filtering. So: a trailing if filters. Items that fail are dropped. Hold on to the word "trailing", because in about ninety seconds I'm going to show you an if that does something completely different.

### Slide 9 — Transforming with a conditional expression

Here's the other tool, and it's a different thing that happens to use the same keyword. It's called a conditional expression, and its shape is: a, if condition, else b. Now the important word there is *expression*. An expression is something that produces a value. So "even if n is divisible by two else odd" isn't a statement that does something — it's a thing that *evaluates to* either the string even or the string odd. And because it produces a value, you can use it anywhere a value is allowed, including at the front of a comprehension. Look at the example: labels equals, bracket, "even" if n modulo two equals zero else "odd", for n in range five. And that gives you the list: even, odd, even, odd, even. Now compare this carefully to the previous slide. On the previous slide, with the filter, some numbers got *dropped* — we asked for range ten and got back only five items. Here, nothing is dropped. We asked for range five and we got back five items. Every single item made it through; each one just got *transformed* into one thing or another on the way. That's the distinction. A filter removes. A conditional expression converts. And because they both use the word if, people mix them up constantly — so let's tackle that head on right now.

### Slide 10 — The position rule

This slide is the one to photograph, honestly. Same keyword, two completely different jobs, and the thing that decides which job you get is *where you put it*. Look at the two lines. The first one: bracket, n, for n in xs, if n is greater than zero. The if is at the end, after the for. That's a filter — it keeps the positive numbers and throws the rest away. You might get back fewer items than you started with. The second line: bracket, n if n is greater than zero else zero, for n in xs. The if is at the front, before the for. That's a transform — every number comes through, but the negative ones are converted to zero on the way. You get back exactly as many items as you started with. Same length, different values. So here's the rule, and I'd like you to say it in your head with me: **if after the for means filter; if-else before the for means transform**. And here's a lovely little tell that'll save you every time — look at the else. A filter has no else. It can't have one, and Python will reject it if you try, because "keep this one, else... what?" is a meaningless question. A transform *must* have an else, because it has to produce a value in both cases. So if you're ever staring at a comprehension wondering which one you're looking at: find the else. No else, it's a filter. Got an else, it's a transform. That one tell resolves ninety per cent of the confusion here.

### Slide 11 — Set comprehensions

Now, remember I said the square brackets were Python telling you what kind of thing you'd get back? Let's change them. If you swap the square brackets for curly braces, you don't get a list any more — you get a set. And everything you learned last week about sets still applies: it holds unique items, and it has no guaranteed order. Look at the example: tags equals, curly brace, tag, for t in tasks dot values, for tag in t's tags. That walks every task, and then walks every tag inside each task, and collects them all into a set. And because it's a set, all the duplicates collapse on their own. If five of your tasks are tagged "home", "home" appears in that set exactly once. You don't write any dedupe logic — the container does it for you, just by being a set. Now, the second bullet there is a genuine practical warning, so please note it. A set has no order. None. That means when you print a set, the order you see is not something you can rely on — in fact, if you print the same set twice in two different runs of your program, you may well see the items in a different order each time. That surprises people and they think their code is broken. It isn't. It's just what a set is. So the rule is: a set is perfect for *deciding what's in it*, but if you want to *display* it to a human, wrap it in sorted, and then you'll get a stable, alphabetical, predictable list every single time. I'll show you exactly that in the demo.

### Slide 12 — Dict comprehensions

And the third flavour. If curly braces give you a set, what gives you a dictionary? Curly braces *plus a colon*. Because a dictionary needs two things per entry — a key and a value — and the colon is how you've been separating those since last week. Look at the example: titles equals, curly brace, tid colon t's title, for tid comma t in tasks dot items. And that builds a fresh dictionary mapping every id to that task's title. So the shape is key, colon, value, and then your for. Everything before the for is what goes *in*; everything from the for onwards is where it *comes from*. And notice dot items there — that's the method from last week that hands you both halves of each entry at once, the key and the value, which is exactly what you need when you're rebuilding a dictionary from another dictionary. So there's the whole family, and it's really one idea wearing three hats: square brackets give you a list, curly braces give you a set, curly braces with a colon give you a dict. Learn the shape once and you get all three.

### Slide 13 — Nested comprehensions

One more piece and then we'll go and run all of this. You can put more than one for in a comprehension, and that's how you walk something nested — a list of lists, or in our case a bunch of tasks that each have a list of tags. But the ordering catches everybody out once, so let me try to save you that hour. Look at the example: flat equals, bracket, n, for row in matrix, for n in row. There are two fors, and the question is which one runs first. And the answer is delightfully simple: **they read left to right, in exactly the same order you'd write the nested loops**. I've put the unrolled version right underneath so you can see them side by side. For row in matrix, then inside that, for n in row, then append. The comprehension has those two fors in exactly the same sequence — outer loop first, inner loop second. So if you ever can't remember, just write the nested loops out in your head and then read them off left to right. And here's what happens when you get it backwards, which I'll show you live: you'll get a NameError, because you'll be trying to use a variable in the inner loop before the outer loop has created it. Python isn't being difficult — you literally asked for the tags of a task before you'd picked a task. So: left to right, outer to inner. Right, let's go and see all of this working.

## Segment 4 · enumerate / zip / reversed / sorted(key=...) (0:57–1:17)

### Slide 19 — Looping idioms

Comprehensions were about the loops you can delete entirely. This next part is about the loops you're going to keep — because you will keep plenty — and four built-in helpers that remove the fiddly bookkeeping from inside them. These four are used absolutely everywhere in real Python code, and the last one, sorted with a key, is one I'd put in the top five most useful things in the language.

### Slide 20 — enumerate, index and value

Here's a need you have all the time: you're walking a list, and you want to know both the item *and* what number it is — so you can print a numbered list, for instance. And the way people coming from other languages do this, almost every time, is the code in the first block. Set n to one before the loop. Print n and the title. Then n plus-equals one at the bottom. And it works! But that counter variable is pure bookkeeping — it's three extra lines that have nothing to do with your actual task, and it's a lovely place for bugs to hide. Forget the increment, and every task is number one. Put the increment in the wrong place, and you're off by one. Python's answer is the second block: for n comma t in enumerate of tasks, start equals one. And that's it. Enumerate takes your list and hands you back pairs — a number and an item, a number and an item — and the for loop unpacks each pair into n and t, using exactly the tuple unpacking you learned in Week 6. No counter to declare, no counter to increment, no counter to get wrong. And do notice that start equals one. By default enumerate starts counting at zero, because that's where Python starts counting everything. But when you're printing a list for a *human*, humans start at one — nobody wants to see "task zero" at the top of their to-do list. So start equals one is the thing you'll want almost every time you're displaying something.

### Slide 21 — zip, walk two lists together

Next: zip. And the name is a beautiful bit of naming, because it does exactly what the zip on your jacket does — it takes two separate rows of teeth and interleaves them into pairs. So if you've got a list of titles and a separate list of due dates, and you want to walk them side by side, zip is your tool. Look at the code: for title comma day in zip of titles and due dates, print the title padded out and "due" and the day. Zip hands you the first title with the first date, then the second with the second, and so on, and the for loop unpacks each pair. Now, two things you need to know about zip, and the second one has bitten every Python developer at least once. The first: zip stops at the shortest. If you give it a list of five titles and a list of three dates, you get three pairs, and the last two titles are simply dropped. Silently. No error, no warning, nothing. And that's often exactly what you want. But sometimes those lists were supposed to be the same length, and the fact that they aren't is a real bug in your data — and zip will quietly hide it from you. Which is why there's a third thing on that slide: you can pass strict equals True. And then, if the lengths don't match, zip raises a ValueError and tells you so, instead of silently truncating. So my advice is: if the two lists are *meant* to line up — and they usually are — pass strict equals True, and let Python tell you when your assumption is wrong. It costs you nothing and it turns a silent wrong answer into a loud, obvious error, which is always the better trade.

### Slide 22 — reversed, back to front

Third helper, and it's the simplest of the four: reversed. For name in reversed of names walks your list from the last item to the first. That's the whole feature. But there are two small things worth knowing. The first: reversed doesn't hand you back a list. It hands you back a kind of lazy view — an object that will produce the items in reverse order as you ask for them, but which doesn't build a whole new list up front. So if you use it in a for loop, as in that example, it just works and you'll never think about it. But if you try to *print* it directly, you won't see your items — you'll see something that looks like "list reverse iterator object at" and a memory address, which looks like an error but isn't. It's just Python saying "I haven't done the work yet, because you haven't asked me to walk it." Wrap it in the word list, and you'll see your items: list of reversed of one, two, three gives you three, two, one. And the second thing, which is the good news: reversed does not touch your original list. Your list is exactly as it was afterwards. That's worth pointing out because the *other* reversing tool, the dot reverse method, does mutate your list in place — and that difference between "hands me a new thing" and "changes my thing" is a theme that's about to come up again, more importantly.

### Slide 23 — sorted with a key

Right. This is the big one. You already know sorted from Week 6 — hand it a list, get back a sorted copy. But sorted has a keyword argument called key, and key is what turns it from useful into powerful. Here's the idea. Your tasks are dictionaries. What does it even mean to sort a dictionary against another dictionary? Is one dict bigger than another? The question doesn't really have an answer, and if you just call sorted on them Python will refuse. So instead, you hand sorted a little function that says: *for each item, here's the thing I actually want you to compare*. That's all a key is. It's a function that takes one item and returns the thing to sort on. Look at the first line: sorted of tasks, key equals lambda t colon t's title. That lambda — and this is exactly the lambda you met in Week 5, doing real work at last — takes one task and returns its title. So sorted goes through, calls that little function on every task, gets a title back for each one, and sorts the tasks by those titles. The tasks themselves come back whole; only the *order* changed. Second line: key equals str dot lower. That's a named function rather than a lambda, and it says "compare them as if they were lowercase" — and I'll show you in the demo why that matters more than you'd think. Third line, and this is the one that feels like a magic trick the first time: key equals lambda t colon the tuple of t's done and t's title. When your key returns a *tuple*, Python sorts by the first element, and then uses the second element only to break ties. So that single line says "put the unfinished tasks first, and then, within each group, sort alphabetically by title" — which is a genuinely sophisticated sort, expressed in about eleven words. Two more things on this slide. Reverse equals True flips the whole order. And the last bullet: Python's sort is *stable*, which is a guaranteed promise, not an accident. It means that when two items have the same key, they keep the order they were already in. That's why the tuple trick works so predictably, and it's why you can sort by one thing, then sort by another, and the first sort still shows through within the ties.

### Slide 24 — sorted versus dot sort

Now, a trap. There are two ways to sort a list and they are not the same, and mixing them up produces one of the great classic Python beginner bugs. Look at the screen. I have nums equals three, one, two. I call sorted of nums, and I get back one, two, three. Then I ask for nums, and — look — it's still three, one, two. Untouched. Because sorted *returns a new sorted list* and leaves your original completely alone. Now the second half. I call nums dot sort. And notice the comment: it returns None. Not a sorted list — None. Because dot sort doesn't hand you anything back; it reorders the list in place. And when I then ask for nums, it is now one, two, three. It changed underneath me. So here's the bug you are absolutely going to write at some point, and I want you to recognise it when you do: you'll write "my list equals my list dot sort", thinking that's how you sort it, and then your list will be None. Not empty — None. And you'll stare at it wondering where your data went. The data went nowhere; you just assigned the return value of a method that doesn't return anything. So: sorted copies and gives it to you. Dot sort mutates and gives you nothing. If you want a new sorted list, use sorted. If you truly want to reorder in place, call dot sort on its own line and don't assign it to anything. This is exactly the same distinction as reversed versus dot reverse from two slides ago — it's a pattern that runs right through Python, and once you spot it you'll spot it everywhere.

### Slide 25 — Generator expressions, a peek

Last concept before the demo, and I'm deliberately giving you this one lightly, because the full story is Week 15. Take a comprehension and change the square brackets to round ones. It looks almost identical — sum of, bracket, n times n for n in range one to six — and it gives you fifty-five, the sum of the first five squares. But something quite different happened underneath. With square brackets, Python would build the complete list of all five squares in memory, hand that finished list to sum, and sum would add it up. With round brackets, no list is ever built at all. Instead Python creates a generator, which produces one square at a time, on demand, as sum asks for them. Sum takes a one, adds it, asks for the next, gets a four, adds it, and so on — and at no point does the whole collection exist anywhere. Now with five numbers that difference is meaningless. But imagine ten million numbers: the list version needs memory for ten million items, and the generator version needs memory for one item at a time. That's the difference between a program that works and a program that falls over. So the rule of thumb for now — and this is all you need this week — is: if you're feeding the result straight into something that consumes it, like sum or max or any, use the round brackets and skip building the list. If you need to keep the collection around and use it more than once, build the real list. We'll go much deeper into generators, and into yield, in Week 15. For now, just recognise the shape when you see it. Right — let's go and use all four helpers on our tasks.

Three, Call plumber. Four, Archive photos. A clean numbered list, and look at the code — there's no counter anywhere. And do notice I wrote tasks dot values there, not just tasks. That matters, and it's worth thirty seconds. If you iterate a dictionary directly, Python hands you its *keys* — so I'd have got the numbers one, two, three, four, which are our task ids, and then t's title would have blown up with a TypeError, because you can't ask an integer for its title. Dot values is what says "give me the task dictionaries themselves". You'll hit that one in your lab if you forget, and now you'll know exactly what it's telling you. No n equals one before the loop, no n plus-equals one at the bottom. Enumerate handed me the number and the task together and the for loop unpacked them. And if I'd left off start equals one, that list would begin at zero — try that in your lab and you'll see immediately why we pass start equals one whenever a human is going to read the output. Now zip. Here's my list of titles, and here's a separate list of due dates — Monday, Tuesday. Let me zip them and turn it into a list so we can see the pairs: and there they are, Buy milk with Monday, Write report with Tuesday. Each title married up with its date, in order. Now watch this, because this is the part I want to stick. My titles list has three items but my dates list only has two. Let me zip them anyway. And look — I get two pairs. Call plumber has simply vanished. No error. No warning. Python didn't think anything was wrong, because as far as zip is concerned, stopping at the shortest is its job. But in a real program that's a task that silently didn't get shown to the user, and you would have no idea. So let me do it again with strict equals True. And now: ValueError, zip argument two is shorter than argument one. Python is telling me exactly what's wrong, loudly, at the moment it happens. That is so much better than a quietly missing task. So please — when your lists are supposed to be the same length, say so with strict equals True, and let Python check your assumption for you.

### Slide 29 — Sorting by a key

Now sorting, and I'm going to start with a small surprise. Let me sort a plain list of three words: banana, apple, and Cherry with a capital C. Just sorted, no key. And look what comes back: Cherry, apple, banana. Cherry is *first*. Now that looks broken, but it isn't — Python is sorting these strings by their character codes, and every capital letter sorts before every lowercase letter. Capital C is code sixty-seven; lowercase a is code ninety-seven. So Cherry genuinely does come first by that rule. It's correct, it's just not what a human means by alphabetical. Now let me add the key: sorted, same list, key equals str dot lower. And now: apple, banana, Cherry. Exactly what a person expects. And here's what I want you to notice — the words came back with their original capitalisation. Cherry still has its capital C. The key function didn't *change* my data; it only told sorted what to *compare*. It lowercased them privately, just for the purposes of deciding the order, and then handed me back my original strings in that order. That's the mental model for key: it's a lens you look through while comparing, not an edit. Now let me do it on the real tasks. Sorted of tasks dot values, key equals lambda t colon t's title. And there's my task list in alphabetical order, whole task dictionaries, just rearranged. And now the tuple key: key equals lambda t colon the tuple of t's done and t's title. Run it, and look at the result — all the unfinished tasks first, alphabetically among themselves, and then all the finished ones, alphabetically among themselves. Two-level sorting from one short line, because a tuple key sorts on the first thing and falls back to the second to break ties. And that works because False sorts before True — remember from Week 2 that booleans really are integers underneath, so False is zero and True is one, and zero comes first. Which is exactly what we want: not-done before done. Finally, the trap, live. Nums equals three, one, two. Sorted of nums gives me one, two, three — and nums itself is still three, one, two, untouched. But nums dot sort — look, the REPL prints nothing at all, because it returned None — and now nums is one, two, three. It changed in place. So if you ever assign the result of dot sort to a variable, you'll be holding None, and now you know exactly why.

### Slide 30 — What you just saw

Let's take stock of that one. Enumerate numbered our list with no counter variable and no chance of an off-by-one, and start equals one made it read the way a human expects. Zip walked two lists in step and quietly dropped an item when the lengths didn't match — and strict equals True turned that silent data loss into a clear ValueError. We saw sorted with a key change the order without touching the data, and we saw why str dot lower is usually what you actually mean when you say alphabetical. We saw a tuple key sort on two levels at once, unfinished tasks first and alphabetical within each group. And we saw the classic trap with our own eyes: sorted hands you back a new list, dot sort hands you back None. Those four helpers, plus comprehensions, are most of what makes Python code look like Python. Now let me spend a few minutes on the other half of that skill, which is knowing when to stop.

### Slide 31 — When not to

I've spent an hour and a half showing you how to compress code, so I want to spend the next few minutes arguing against myself — because the most common mistake people make after this session is going back to their code and comprehension-ing absolutely everything in sight. ### Slide 32 — The readability rule

Look at the two examples. The first one: the title of each task, for each task, where it's not done. That's fine. It's one idea, it reads like a sentence, you can say it out loud in one breath. Ship it. Now the second one. Two fors, two ifs, a function call on the front, and a nested call in the middle. Technically that's completely valid Python, and it probably works. But I cannot read it. I have to sit down and decode it. And if I have to decode it today, then you, six months from now, at five o'clock on a Friday, trying to fix a bug in it — you have no chance. That should be a loop. It'll be five lines instead of one, and every one of those five lines will be obvious. So here's the test I'd like you to actually use, and it's the first bullet: if you can't read it out loud in one breath, write the loop. It sounds glib but it's a genuinely good heuristic, and I use it. The moment you find yourself pausing halfway through a comprehension to work out what it's doing, that comprehension has failed at its only job, which is clarity. The second bullet is about purpose. A comprehension exists to *build a collection*. That's its one job. So if what you actually want is to print each item, or write each item to a file, or send each item somewhere — you don't want a collection at all, you want a loop. And I want to name the specific abuse here because I see it constantly: people write a list comprehension purely to call print inside it, and then just throw the resulting list of Nones away. Please don't do that. It builds a whole list of nothings for no reason, and it hides the fact that you're doing something with side effects inside something that looks like a value. If you're doing, loop. If you're building, comprehend. That's the line. And honestly, the fact that you now *can* write a nested triple comprehension doesn't mean you have to prove it. The best Python developers I know write remarkably plain code. Right — that's the taught content for today. Let me take questions on any of it, and then I'll walk you through this week's lab.
